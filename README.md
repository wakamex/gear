# gear

gear is a voice front-end for coding agents. You speak to an agent such as [Claude Code](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-code) or [Codex](https://github.com/openai/codex), gear sends the message when you stop talking, reads the reply aloud and listens again, all on your own machine. This README records what works and the decisions behind it.

gear is a C program on [gesso](https://github.com/wakamex/gesso), which supplies the window, audio, echo cancellation and downloads, and [hearsay](https://github.com/wakamex/hearsay), which supplies speech detection, recognition and synthesis. It runs on Linux and Windows, on the GPU where one is usable and on the CPU otherwise; the Windows build is 50 MB with echo cancellation, most of it the GPU's compiled shaders, and 6.2 MB built for the CPU only.

## What works

The spoken loop runs end to end, from microphone to message and from reply text to the speakers:

- Listening: each 10 ms of microphone audio goes through gesso's `gs_voice` for echo cancellation, and [Silero VAD](https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad) decides when speech starts and stops. Speech is cut into pieces at pauses of half a second, each piece keeping the 0.5 s before its start, and a recognizer thread transcribes each piece with Parakeet as it ends. The piece being spoken is also transcribed again every 0.25 s while it lasts, so its words show as they are said; the piece's own transcription replaces that draft, and only it goes through the checks below and into the message. Where Parakeet runs on a GPU, it writes the drafts itself, when no finished piece is waiting. On the CPU a draft already running would hold up the next piece's text by its own length (about 3 s for 10 s of speech with parakeet-ft-v1 on one thread), so the 110M model writes them instead, on a thread of its own, when `parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m-Q4_K_M.gguf` is in the models folder (it is not downloaded, since only a machine without a usable GPU needs it); without it there are no drafts. In the tests a 4.4 s sentence played in real time showed 4 drafts from the recognizer itself, and 15 from the 110M model on its own thread, the last close to the whole sentence either way. A piece whose loudest 100 ms stays under 0.0015 of full scale is not transcribed, since Parakeet writes words such as "Yeah" or "absolutely" into breathing; of the 119 kept pieces it found words in, the 9 under that level were all of that kind, and sentences peaked ten times higher (median). A piece Parakeet did write words into is then scored as speech or as another sound of the voice, since it also writes "Okay" into a throat clear, "um" into a cough and "oh" into a sigh, and a piece that scores under the cut is dropped. On speakers the score was not fitted on, the cut drops 2.0% of speech, heard as recorded or in a room, and lets through 33% of those sounds; of 81 pieces of three words or more from real use, it drops 1. When the speakers played anything during a piece, their sound over the same stretch is transcribed after it, and a piece of which at least three words in four are in theirs is dropped as a reply, video or call heard through the speakers; no piece's text reaches the message box before that check. Where the speakers cannot be recorded, what gear itself played stands in for their sound, so the check then covers gear's own replies. In the tests, a reply that reaches the microphone past the canceller stops itself, and the words heard of it ("Here is the" or "Here is the plan", from run to run) make a message without the check and none with it. Pieces add up to one message; once the user has stopped and every piece has its text, a countdown (3 s by default) runs, and talking again stops it.
- Speaking: a reply is synthesized by Kyutai's [Pocket TTS](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts) on a thread of its own and played as it is made, through a `gs_stream` in gesso's mix, in one of two of its voices, alba or mary; a click on the voice in the status bar switches to the other from the next sentence on, and the choice is remembered in `voice.txt` in the models folder. The two were picked by ear from Pocket's 27 English voices, all reading the same five agent replies. Gradium's [Phonon](https://huggingface.co/gradium/phonon-31m-en), built on Pocket TTS at half its size, got as many words across on 300 sentences from a week of agent replies (10.10% of words wrong against 10.50% for Pocket with alba, within noise, transcribed by parakeet-ft-v1 with gear's reading rules below), but every one of its 12 American voices stressed "record" as the noun in "the pipeline doesn't record", and alba took about 10% less time to read the 300. In the tests, mary's speech has a median pitch about 1.3 times alba's, and switching back gives alba's again. hearsay puts a comma before each piece Pocket generates, which keeps a short first word: without it Pocket dropped an opening "I" from 4.4% of replies and from 8.1% of the later pieces of long ones.
- Interrupting: when the user talks for 0.3 s during a reply, the reply stops. A shorter burst does not stop it: through loudspeakers a low vowel of the reply itself can reach the microphone in a way the echo canceller does not model, for about 0.15 s, and stopping at the first 50 ms of speech cut replies off a sentence in. In the tests, the reply stops 0.33 s after the user starts talking with headphones and 0.42 s after over loudspeakers with the echo canceller, and the player is silent 10 ms later.
- Echo: with `-Decho=true`, gesso's WebRTC echo canceller is given everything the speakers play, recorded with gesso's `gs_loopback` (WASAPI loopback on Windows, the default output's monitor on Linux), so neither a reply nor a podcast or call from another app is taken for the user talking. Where the speakers cannot be recorded (macOS, for now), it is given what gear plays. The status bar says which: "echo cancel: all audio" or "echo cancel: gear's audio". In the tests, where the microphone hears the reply at half its level 100 ms late as well as the user, the reply plays on until the user talks; without the canceller it cuts itself off 0.7 to 0.9 s in. The volume stops at 200% because of what the canceller does to the user's voice above it. Measured at 300% on a desktop PC with loudspeakers, the reply reached the microphone as loud as the user's voice at its loudest moments; the canceller took 33 to 37 dB of the reply out while the user was silent, and nothing of the reply became a message, but a sentence said over the reply ("tell me a different story but make it shorter") came out as "I'm sorry that maybe actually" and did not stop the reply. With a phrase of the user's voice mixed into that recording at 13 moments and the recording run through the canceller again, the phrase lost up to 14 dB and was transcribed word for word 7 times at 300%; with the speakers and their echo scaled down to 200% it lost about 3 dB at most (9 times), and to 100% the same (11 times). Without `-Decho`, use headphones.
- Debugging: with `GEAR_DEBUG` set, gear writes what the listener works with into `recordings` in its data folder: 16 kHz WAV files of three channels on one clock (the microphone as recorded, what the speakers played, which the echo canceller works against and which is silent when the speakers are not recorded, and the microphone as cleaned), 5 minutes a file, named for the local time of their first sample, the newest hour kept. A piece in `heard.jsonl` can be found in them to the sample. A reading stopped by talking also leaves its last 4 s of microphone audio beside the program.
- Agents: messages go to the user's coding agents through [Panetone](https://github.com/wakamex/panetone), the router that already carries them between the agents and the user's Telegram and Signal, and the reply that ends the agent's turn comes back to be read aloud. gear hands Panetone each message as the user, `panetone say --to ROUTE --as CLIENT` (gear for the phone's hub, gear-window, gear-cli), for the route of the agent's tab, so the agent gets it as if typed in its Telegram topic: Panetone delivers it, queues it for an agent in the middle of a turn, and posts a copy, "[via CLIENT] ...", to the topic, so the Telegram chat holds the whole conversation. The answers come through `panetone watch`, everything Panetone posts to the user's channels, which gear follows from where it last stopped (src/panetone.c, shared by the window, the hub and the command line): Panetone follows each agent turn with a `turn_end` line naming the message that answers what began it, and of the posts of the agent talked to, that one is the reply; the others are its notes along the way. The agents to pick from are [Wakterm](https://github.com/wakamex/wakterm)'s (`wakterm cli agent catalog`, with each agent's busy or idle status), and [endchat](https://github.com/wakamex/endchat)'s profiles: each profile under `~/.endchat` that `endchat serve` is serving, named for the profile, busy or idle as its `state.json` says. `--to` names an agent or, when no agent has that name, a tab by the title it shows. The hub also opens a conversation that is a Panetone route with no agent of its own, such as a [room](https://github.com/wakamex/panetone) whose member routes' agents all see every message: what is sent there goes through `panetone say` to the room, and the room's posts, each member's as `NAME: text`, make up that one conversation. Checked live against the room priv-room, with members priv-astra, priv-opus and priv-neon: a message typed into its conversation reached all three, and their answers came back into it. In a room each post is shown as its speaker's: the hub takes off the "NAME: " Panetone puts before it, names the member by its route (priv-opus rather than its agent priv_test_claude), and gives the app the harness of the member's agent, whose logo (`assets/harness`) the app shows beside it in place of the character. In a test with a stand-in Panetone, `gear send` printed the reply that ended the agent's turn, and the phone's hub showed the agent's progress note and spoke that reply.
- Reading replies: a reply is turned into what is worth hearing before it is spoken, by fixed rules. Code blocks and tables are named rather than read ("A code block of 6 lines.", "A table of 2 rows."), Markdown's marks are dropped, links are read as their text and bare addresses as their site ("a github dot com link"), paths as their file's name, and a file's name without its extension ("NOTES.md" as "NOTES"), which the speech model spelled out, snake_case as words, and headings and list items become sentences. Numbers and the symbols around them are written out as words, since the speech model reads digits unreliably (Pocket TTS read 139 as "39" and 0.34 as "through"): integers, decimals and versions, codes with leading zeros digit by digit, years in pairs, times, dates, percent, money in dollars and cents, units, and a hex hash as its first six characters. On the 300 sentences above, this cut the words Pocket TTS got wrong from 14.88% to 10.88%. The word "read" is written "red" where grammar says it is the past: after a form of be or have ("is read aloud", "has never read") and after he, she or it ("She read only the first page"), unless a verb such as let or did comes before. Phonon, gear's speech model at the time, said "reed" in three such sentences 16 times of 18, and "red" when it was written so 11 times of 12, judged by the vowel's first resonance against the same sentence written with "red" and with "reed". Elsewhere the word is left to the model, which said it correctly 30 times of 30 in five other sentences ("I read the whole file and found", "The agent read the file and stopped", "I will read"). The whole reply is read, however long; stopping it is a tap or a key away. Words the speech model says wrongly every time (Pocket TTS reads only letters, and said "proton" as "PRO-tuhn" and "meson" as "MEE-sun" in each of 8 tries) can be taught a spelling to be said as, such as "Pro-tawn": the list is kept in pronunciations.txt in gear's data folder, a word and its spelling to a line, and applied to whole words in any case, with a plural's ending and a capital kept. The phone app's settings have a Pronunciation panel to teach one: a word typed is looked up in [CMUdict](https://github.com/cmusphinx/cmudict), the Carnegie Mellon pronouncing dictionary (downloaded with the models), whose way of saying it is offered respelled in syllables ("proh-tahn"), or a spelling of one's own is typed, and either can be heard before it is kept, beside the word as the speech says it by itself, without the taught spellings ('W'), to compare. `gear read` prints what would be said of a reply.
- Models: the window downloads any that are missing, showing its progress, and `gear fetch` does the same from the command line: five files, 638 MB in all, from Hugging Face at pinned commits, resuming a broken download and keeping a file only when its size and SHA-256 match the published ones.

| File | What it is | Size |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| `parakeet-ft-v1-Q4_K_M.gguf` | [parakeet-ft-v1](https://huggingface.co/freetranscribe/parakeet-ft-v1), a fine-tune of NVIDIA's Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3, speech recognition (CC-BY-4.0) | 485 MB |
| `pocket-tts-english_2026-09-Q8_0.gguf` | Kyutai Pocket TTS, English speech synthesis (CC-BY-4.0) | 138 MB |
| `alba.safetensors`, `mary.safetensors` | Kyutai's alba and mary voices for Pocket TTS (CC-BY-4.0) | 6 MB each |
| `silero-vad-v6.2-F32.gguf` | Silero VAD v6.2, telling speech from other sound (MIT) | 1.2 MB |

- Window: `gear window` draws the loop in the toolcore-native style: the state (LISTENING, HEARING, SENDING, WAITING, SPEAKING, MUTED or STOPPED), the target agent, the countdown's length with `-` and `+` beside it to change it a second at a time (0 to 10 s, where 0 sends a message as soon as its transcript is in, after the half-second pause that ends a piece; remembered), a volume slider for everything gear plays (0 to 200%, dragged, clicked or turned with the wheel over it, remembered), a microphone meter, the message with its countdown, under the messages sent before (a long message takes up to half the window and shows its newest words; the wheel scrolls back through it and the earlier messages, each under the time it was sent and its agent, the panel taking half the window meanwhile, and the box's title says how many are above), the reply as written with code and tables boxed as not read, and a status bar that turns rust with a fault. Space mutes: the microphone pauses and the pipeline hears silence instead, so a piece of speech under way ends and a counting message is still sent. Esc stops the reading or drops the message, Enter sends now, and `+` and `-` change the text size. `gear window --demo --shot F.png` draws a made-up exchange to a picture without a display.
- Messages sent: each is kept as a line of JSON in `messages.jsonl` in gear's data folder, with the time and the agent, and the newest 200 are shown again after a restart.
- Remembered target: the agent the window last reached with `--to`, and the host it was on, are kept in `target.txt` in the models folder, so starting gear with no options, from a shortcut or the taskbar, goes back to them; `--to` overrides them, and `--to none` forgets them.
- Agents on another machine: with `GEAR_WAKTERM_HOST` naming it (or the host remembered with the agent), gear runs `wakterm` and `panetone` there through `ssh -T`, the message as a quoted argument, since the Windows ssh client never passes on the end of piped input, and follows `panetone watch` there through ssh too, starting it again from the last line read when the connection drops. Each answer is read until it is a whole JSON object, because a session can stay open after the command has exited. gear passes `--no-auto-start` to every `wakterm` call, so a client that cannot find the server fails rather than starting a second one.
- Updates: a published build is a test build, and says so in its window title and status bar with its build name. On its first run it asks whether to keep itself up to date, and until the answer is yes it never looks for updates; clicking the build's name in the status bar turns updating on or off. When on, it reads a manifest from the update server every minute with gesso's `gs_update`, naming the build and each file's name, SHA-256 and size, and checks the manifest's [Ed25519](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/) signature against the publisher's key built into the program. Files of a newer build are downloaded, checked against their hashes and put in place of the old ones, which are renamed aside. gear restarts into the new build only when nothing is being heard, sent, waited for or read, there has been no key or click for two minutes, and the last update restart was at least 15 minutes ago; clicking "a new test build is ready" restarts at once. The new build comes back muted if gear was, does not take the keyboard, and says once in the status bar that it was updated. A build that keeps failing in its first minutes puts the one before it back and says so. The server is reached over plain HTTP; the signature, made with a key that never leaves the publisher's machine, is what keeps a changed file out. A build of one's own, without `-Dbuild`, never updates itself.
- Updating a machine at once: `tools/windows-update`, run after `tools/publish-update`, copies the published build to the Windows PC over SSH, ends the running gear and starts the new one in the signed-in user's session with deskrun, without waiting for gear to be idle.
- Test download: gear has no release builds yet, only test builds. [gear-test-windows.zip](https://updates.waka.mx/gear/gear-test-windows.zip) is the newest, built by `tools/publish-update` from each commit. It holds the program, which is the phone's app built for Windows (below, under the phone app) and connects to a computer running gear's hub, its characters' pictures, its font, its LICENSE and THIRD-PARTY-LICENSES.txt with the notices of the code compiled into it. Unpacked into a folder of its own, it asks on first run whether to update itself, and if you say yes it downloads every new version and restarts when you've left it alone.

Not built yet: the reply's sentence being read shown bright, editing a misheard word in the message, changing the target from the window, the tray, and sending to a terminal pane that runs no agent.

## Phone app

The phone app is a character to hold while talking. Held, it listens, and what is said goes as it is said to gear on the computer, over [Secure Shell (SSH)](https://www.openssh.com/); let go, it is transcribed there and sent to the agent, and the agent's reply comes back as text and as speech in alba or mary, played as it is made. Holding it again while it speaks stops the reply. With always-on, switched under the character, it listens without being held: the computer runs the phone's audio through gear's own listener, which finds the messages by their pauses and the countdown as the window does, the phone shows the words as they are said and the countdown, and a tap sends at once or stops a reply. The phone listens while a reply plays too, and talking over it stops it, as in the window: the phone reports how far it has played the reply with each piece of its microphone's audio, so the computer, which made the reply, lines what the phone's speaker played up with what its microphone heard, and gives that to the same echo canceller and the same check against the speakers' words, and the pipeline stops the reply on the phone after 0.3 s of talking over it. The phone also asks Android for its microphone as for a call, with the phone's own echo cancelling. In tests through `gear serve` built with `-Decho=true`, with a simulated phone whose speaker reached its microphone at half volume 100 ms late, a reply played to its end without stopping itself and none of it became a message (3 runs), and a different sentence said over it 1 s in stopped it once and was sent whole (2 runs). The voice at the right of the top line switches voice; under settings is the commit the app was built from (with a + when built from a tree with changes). The computer does the recognition, the agent and the speech with the same models as the window, so the phone only records, plays and draws; the app is 3.5 MB.

- Connection: the app makes its own [Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_Curve_Digital_Signature_Algorithm) P-256 key the first time, kept with gesso's `gs_secret` (sealed by an Android Keystore key), and signs in with it alone. Settings show the key as a line for the computer's `~/.ssh/authorized_keys`, `command="gear serve",restrict ecdsa-sha2-nistp256 ...`, so the key can run gear's server mode and nothing else: no shell, no forwarding. The computer's own key is pinned the first time it is seen, and a different one later stops the connection. SSH is [libssh2](https://libssh2.org/) over [Mbed TLS](https://www.trustedfirmware.org/projects/mbed-tls/), compiled from their sources by `build.zig`; the app signs with Mbed TLS itself, since libssh2's Mbed TLS code reads a private key from memory only when the byte after it is zero, which it never writes.
- Server: `gear serve` reads and writes framed messages on standard input and output, as `src/serve.h` describes, with the computer's models folder, remembered agent and voice. Through SSH to this computer, a sentence held for 4.4 s came back transcribed 0.15 s after it was let go, and its reply finished speaking 0.24 s after that, on an RTX 3090 once the GPU had compiled its shaders; the first run took 5.9 s for them.
- Agents from the phone: the hub sends as gear and shows on the phone everything the feed brings, as Telegram shows it: what the agent talked to answers (spoken), its notes along the way and other agents' posts (shown), and the user's messages sent through another client (shown as theirs); a copy of a message sent from the phone itself is not shown twice. The window shows only the agent it talks to, and follows the feed from when it starts, so replies that came while it was closed are not read when it opens.
- Hub: `gear hub`, kept running as a systemd user service (`tools/gear-hub.service`, copied to `~/.config/systemd/user/` and enabled), holds the models, the agent and what waits for the phone between connections, on a socket in the user's runtime folder, for up to eight clients at once (the phone, and the same app as the desktop's gear on Windows); `gear serve` then only passes the phone's connection to it, so a phone connecting is answered in 0.03 s instead of after a second of loading. Every message the phone is given (what the user said, replies, posts) is numbered in a log on disk (hub-log.jsonl, the newest 1,000), and the phone says on each connection the number of the newest it has, so the hub gives it again whatever came after: what came while no phone was connected, and what went to a connection that had died without the hub knowing, which installing a new version of the app leaves behind. The phone tells the hub when its app is behind others, and new replies then come as text alone. The hub also follows Panetone's `panetone watch`, everything Panetone posts to Mihai's own Telegram and Signal channels, and gives those posts to the phone the same way, as Telegram shows them, keeping its place in the feed across restarts. In a test with a stand-in agent, a phone that hung up after its message's number came was given the reply on its next connection, and a third connection, which had everything, was given nothing again. With several clients, every message goes to all of them, each marked with its conversation (the user's message and the reply as such, 'M' and 'R', only to the client they belong to, and to the others as messages of that agent's conversation, so a client with another conversation open files them there), and speech, what the agent is doing and always-on listening go to the one talked, typed or asked to speak on last, which also picks the agent messages go to: each client sends to the agent of the conversation it has open. When that client goes, the one used last before it takes over. In a test with two stand-in clients, each got its own greeting, speech went only to the one that asked for it and moved when the other asked, a typed message reached both, and each one's message went to its own conversation's agent.
- Conversations: each agent has its own conversation, named by the title of its tab, as a Telegram group has one topic for each subject. The page under the character shows the open one, both sides, and the name at the top opens the list: conversations with messages, the newest first, each with its count of unseen replies and its newest message, then the agents without one yet, all narrowed by a search field. Each agent's entry has a dot: amber and pulsing while it is in the middle of a turn, green when it is done, as Wakterm reports it, and the open conversation's takes the place of the connection's dot at the top left while connected (amber while connecting and rust when down, otherwise); both are asked again every 3 s while shown. Opening a conversation sends to its agent from then on. The count beside the open conversation's name at the top is of the replies unseen in the others, and a tap on it opens the next one in the list that has any. A swipe anywhere outside the conversation's page goes to the next conversation in the list (left or up) or the one before (right or down), the way pagers on Android and iOS do it: the screen follows the finger once it has moved 16 points along one axis, with the conversation beside it coming in, and on letting go it goes on after a fling (25 points and 400 points a second that way) or past half way and goes back otherwise, easing out over at most 600 ms; with no conversation that way it stretches like a rubber band. A finger kept still on the character for 0.25 s is talking instead. Switching while an agent still owes a reply does not drop it: the hub keeps waiting on that agent and gives its reply to the phone as text for its own conversation, which counts it unseen. In a test with two stand-in agents, a reply that came 7 s after switching away from its agent reached the phone, labeled with that agent's conversation. History (the last 2,000 messages) is kept on each device across restarts. The unseen counts and the muted conversations are the hub's, the same on every device: it counts an agent's message unseen when no device in front of the user has its conversation open, a conversation opened on any device has its replies seen on all of them (and its phone notification taken back), and it keeps both in unseen.txt and muted.txt in gear's data folder, sending them to every device whenever they change ('U'). The phone shows no notification for a muted conversation, nor for Panetone's receipts and notices. A new message scrolls the page to its start only while the newest message shows; read further back, the page stays where it is and a button at its lower right goes to the newest, counting what came meanwhile, and speech that starts by itself is not followed there. Only the messages within a screen's height of what shows are laid out and drawn, the rest taking the height they had when last laid out, so scrolling costs the same however long the conversation: drawn on this computer's CPU, a frame took 97 ms with 800 messages before and 33 ms after, as with one.
- Following along: while a message is read aloud, the sentence being heard is drawn brighter on an amber tint (a table or code block, read as its count, is tinted whole), and the conversation glides to keep it a third of the way down; a finger on the conversation stops that, and a follow button brings it back, opening the conversation being read ("follow in gitrep") when another is open. The computer synthesizes the reply sentence by sentence and tells the phone, before each sentence's speech, which bytes of the message it was read from and how far into the speech it starts ('L'), since Pocket TTS gives no timing of its own within what it is given; gear_speakable keeps, for each byte it says, the place in the reply it was read from, through Markdown's marks, numbers said in words and taught pronunciations. The phone marks the last sentence whose start its player has passed. A reply whose speech comes while another message is still being read is marked from when its own speech starts.
- On the desktop: the same app is the Windows program (`gear-phone.exe`, published as `gear.exe`), a client of the hub beside the phone, using the computer's microphone and speakers as the phone uses its own, with text at gink's sizes (15 points, 13 for the dim secondary text) where the phone's is 18; it updates itself as the window before it did. With a keyboard, holding Space talks as holding the character does (a tap stops what is being said, or in always-on sends now) and Escape stops a reply, or leaves the settings; Alt+E opens the conversation list and closes it again; however the list is opened, with a keyboard its search field has the keyboard at once, so typing searches. Alt+Up and Alt+Down go to the conversation before and after the open one in the list, as a swipe does. A conversation chosen from it (Enter takes the first match), or gone to with F1, F2, Alt+Up or Alt+Down, gives the box to type in the keyboard, so typing goes to its agent at once. As in wakterm, F1 goes to the conversation with the newest unseen reply and F2 to the one with the oldest, and F3 mutes the open conversation or unmutes it, as holding a finger on a conversation in the list for half a second does: a muted conversation shows a struck-through bell after its name at the top and in the list, F1 and F2 pass it by, its unseen replies are left out of the count at the top (its own count in the list is dimmed), and a tap on that count does not go to it. Its settings, which scroll, hold the volume of what it plays and, for every device at once, always-on's countdown (0 to 10 s, changed on each tap without waiting for the hub) and the recognition model in use (a tap loads the next one in the models folder), with where it runs and whether the echo canceller is built in; a small meter beside the hint shows the microphone's level while it is open.
- Characters: four drawn characters to choose from in the settings (a tomato, a fox, a girl and a robot, made with Codex's image generation as `assets/avatars/README.md` records), each with frames at rest, blinking, speaking and listening, and four of thinking, looking up one way and the other with a hand at the chin, eyes closed and about to speak, played in turn while gear hears, sends or waits for the agent.
- Tucked away: a handle at the middle of the line above the conversation tucks the character away, the room it took gliding shut in a fifth of a second, and a tap on it there brings it back; the choice is kept on each device. Tucked away, the character sits small at the left of the box to type in and does all it did: held (or Space) it talks, tapped it stops what is said, and it listens, thinks and speaks as before; what its line said shows in the empty box when it is news ("speaking: tap to stop", "waiting for …"), and always-on is switched in the settings. For when voice is not wanted, or on the desktop, where a large character to click is not needed.
- Typing: a box under the conversation takes a typed message, growing to five lines, up to 64 KB (a paste that does not fit is cut at the limit, which the note line says), sent with its button or Enter (Shift+Enter for a new line); on the phone, Enter starts a new line, as in phones' chat apps, and only the button sends to the open conversation's agent as one said would be ('M'). The + at the box's left picks files in the system's file dialog, on the phone and the desktop, shown above the box until sent (a tap on one takes it off). A file dropped on the desktop window goes the same way, as does an image pasted with Ctrl+V when the clipboard holds no text, such as a screenshot (saved as a PNG under `pasted/` in the app's folder until sent), and on the phone an image put in from the on-screen keyboard, such as a picture from Gboard's clipboard, through the SDL fork's `SDL_HINT_ANDROID_TEXT_INPUT_IMAGES`. Files go to the computer before the message ('F', 8 MB each at most), are kept in gear's data folder under `attachments/DATE/` (a name without an ending, as Android's dialog gives some files, gets the one its first bytes show, such as `.png`), and the agent gets the message with a line for each, `[attached TYPE: PATH]`, as Panetone gives it a file sent from Telegram. In a test with a stand-in Panetone, a picture sent this way was kept byte for byte and named in the message the agent got. An agent's question comes with its answers as buttons under it, as in Telegram: a tap presses that answer through `panetone answer` ('C'), the same as Telegram's button, and the button pressed stays lit; a typed message instead goes to the agent after the question closes. A Claude question form, which asks several questions in one message, keeps its buttons open: each tap chooses an option of its question, Panetone's answer gives back the form's buttons with the chosen ones marked ✓, which every connected app shows lit, and Submit, Chat about this or Cancel close it. While it has the keyboard on the phone, whose on-screen keyboard takes half the screen, the character folds away, so the conversation keeps the room above the keyboard.
- Reading aloud: the agent's reply to a message sent is read aloud as it comes. A setting chooses whether other new messages are read too: "replies to me" (only those), "this conversation" (every new message in the open conversation, such as an agent's post through Panetone), or "all conversations". A reply to a message sent while something older is still being read, such as a long message tapped to hear it, stops that and is read at once; replies that come with no message sent between them wait their turn, so an agent's two replies in a row are both heard whole. Other new messages wait their turn after what is being read, and messages given again on connecting ('Z' with "again") are not read. Panetone's own receipts for a send ("[pending] ...", "[submitted] ...") and its notices, which `panetone watch` labels by kind, are shown dim and folded to their first line, which a tap unfolds, never read aloud and not counted unseen.
- Speaking a message: each message has a speaker left of its time; a tap speaks it, in place of what is being spoken, and a tap on the same one, a square while it plays, stops it. The reply being spoken as it comes has the square too. Speech goes on with the phone locked or the app behind others: the app keeps running there (SDL's `SDL_HINT_ANDROID_BLOCK_ON_PAUSE` off), drawing nothing and without the microphone until it is back. In a test with the app behind, a message's 3.9 s of speech came in 1.1 s, and a stop sent at its first audio ended it.
- Selecting and links: a finger held still on a message's text for half a second selects the word under it, and a drag from there goes on a word at a time, across messages too, and Android's toolbar over the selection copies it, with handles to move its ends and the magnifier while one is dragged; a web address in a message is a link, which a tap opens in the browser.
- Tables: a reply's Markdown tables are drawn as tables, each column as wide as its contents and no wider, and when the table is wider than the screen, each column keeps its widest word and shares the rest by how much more it needs, wrapping its cells; the text shrinks, down to 11 points, only when the words alone do not fit. A column is aligned as the colons of the line under its header say (`:--` left, `:-:` centred, `--:` right), and without them a column of numbers is set to the right; its header and its wrapped cells are aligned with it, a line at a time. A tap or click on a table shows it at its full size over the screen, its columns as wide as their contents on one line (a column of long text at most most of the screen's width, wrapped) and its text at the messages' size, dragged or scrolled (Shift across) to its edges, and closed by its button, back or Escape. The talk screen uses the phone's whole width, since what Android's safe area keeps clear at the sides in portrait covers nothing that text minds.
- In the background: the app stays connected behind other apps through gesso's foreground service, as gink does, its connection reconnecting by itself five seconds after it breaks, and the connection's own thread shows a notification for each reply or post while Android pauses the app's loop, one for each conversation, holding its newest message; a tap opens that conversation.
- Build: `tools/android-apk arm64-v8a` builds `zig-out/android/gear.apk` with the Android SDK and Native Development Kit (NDK) and no Gradle, as gink does. `gear-phone`, built beside `gear` on Linux, is the same app on the computer: `gear-phone --say FILE.wav HOST USER` connects as the phone does, sends a recording and prints what comes back, and `gear-phone --shot FILE setup|talk|pick|table` draws a screen to a picture.

## Build and run

With [Zig](https://ziglang.org/) 0.17.0, which fetches gesso and hearsay at the commits pinned in `build.zig.zon`, and the [glslc](https://github.com/google/shaderc) shader compiler for the GPU build (Fedora's `glslc` package, or `-Dglslc=PATH`):

```sh
zig build -Doptimize=ReleaseFast             # zig-out/bin/gear, 51 MB on Linux, needing only libc
zig build -Doptimize=ReleaseFast -Decho=true # with echo cancellation, for loudspeakers
zig build -Doptimize=ReleaseFast -Dgpu=none  # CPU only, 6.7 MB, no glslc needed
gear fetch                                   # the models, into gear's folder in the user's data folder
gear agents [NAME]                           # the agents Wakterm can reach, or the one NAME reaches
gear listen --to AGENT                       # talk to AGENT; its replies are read aloud
gear send --to AGENT "Some message."         # one message, its reply printed
gear read - < reply.md                       # what would be read aloud of a reply
gear listen --speak-back                     # no agent: each message is printed and read back
gear window --to AGENT                       # the window; GEAR_WAKTERM_HOST=host reaches agents on another machine
```

On Windows gear.exe is a windowed program, so starting it from a shortcut or the taskbar opens no console; run from a terminal, the command-line modes print to it, though the prompt can come back before they finish (pipe the output, for example to `Out-String` in PowerShell, to wait for it). `--cpu` keeps the models on the CPU in a GPU build. `GEAR_MODELS` names another folder for the models. Three modes run the same pipeline on recordings, without a microphone or speakers:

```sh
gear hear speech.wav                         # messages as they would be sent, with their times
gear say "Some reply." reply.wav             # the reply as the player plays it
gear interrupt "Some reply." talking.wav out.wav  # a reply while talking.wav plays as the microphone
```

`zig build test` runs the tests. The listener and file tests always run; with `GEAR_MODELS` set to a folder holding the models, the end-to-end tests also transcribe `tests/utterance.wav` (LibriSpeech test-clean 1089-134686-0010, CC-BY-4.0) through the pipeline and check that talking two seconds into a reply stops it; built with `-Decho=true`, they also check the echo canceller with a simulated loudspeaker. With `GEAR_AGENT` naming an agent kept for tests, they send it a message through Wakterm and check its reply; without it they still check, against the real `wakterm` and an agent that does not exist, that a refused message is reported as refused.

## Decisions

### Window: one compact panel in toolcore-native

The window is to be drawn with gesso's `gs_ui` in the toolcore-native style: warm near-black, one monospace font, square panels, and colour and brightness only for what is happening now. It shows the current exchange only, since the agent's own session keeps the conversation.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ● LISTENING   → pockettts (claude)            ▁▃▅▂ mic   │
├─ MESSAGE ────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ fix the countdown so a long pause doesn't send, and      │
│ then run the tests…                                      │
│ ▰▰▰▰▰▰▰▱▱▱  sending in 2 s   enter sends · esc cancels   │
├─ REPLY ──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ I found the bug. The countdown timer resets whenever…    │
│ ┌ code, not read ────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│ │ if (l->below < l->p.pause_frames) …                │   │
│ └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│ Want me to commit it?                                    │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ echo cancel on · Parakeet 0.07 cpu · Pocket · 0.6 GB     │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

- State line: a dot and one word, LISTENING (green), HEARING, SENDING or SPEAKING (amber), MUTED or an error (rust); the target agent, which a click on it or `/` changes: a picker lists the agents Wakterm knows by the title their tab shows, the ones sent to lately first, and typing narrows them to those whose tab or name has what is typed; Enter takes the first, a click any, and Esc closes it. Messages go to the agent picked from then on, a start without `--to` comes back to it, and a reply the agent before still owes is read when it comes; and a microphone level meter, so the user can see they are heard before the words arrive.
- Message: the words appear as they are said, those of the piece still being spoken dim until its own transcription replaces them, and a piece waiting for its text shows as a dim ellipsis. The countdown is an amber bar draining under the text, with its keys printed beside it. Clicking into the text to fix a misheard word stops the countdown.
- Reply: the sentence being read is bright, what was read is dim, what is to come is muted. Code and tables the reading skips stay visible in a box marked as not read. Esc or talking stops the reading.
- Status bar: whether echo cancellation is on (or that headphones are needed), what is loaded, memory in use; a fault replaces it in rust.
- Keys: Space mutes the microphone, Esc cancels the message or stops the reading, Enter sends now, `/` changes the target, `+` and `-` change the font size.
- Tray: gear can wait in the tray with its icon in the state's colour, and the window can collapse to the state line and message panel as a strip at the screen's edge.

### Speech recognition: parakeet-ft-v1 Q4_K_M on transcribe.cpp

gear recognizes speech with parakeet-ft-v1, a fine-tune of NVIDIA's 0.6B Parakeet v3, running on [transcribe.cpp](https://github.com/handy-computer/transcribe.cpp), a C/C++ speech-to-text library built on the [ggml](https://github.com/ggml-org/ggml) tensor library. It uses the 4-bit Q4_K_M weights from [freetranscribe/parakeet-ft-v1-gguf](https://huggingface.co/freetranscribe/parakeet-ft-v1-gguf), a 485 MB file under CC-BY-4.0, on the GPU where one is usable. Each stretch of speech between pauses is transcribed once, when the pause comes, so recognition does no work while the user is silent.

parakeet-ft-v1 is the most accurate option measured, 0.6 to 1.1 points better than v3 with 95% intervals that exclude zero. On an RTX 3080 it transcribes 10 s of speech in 0.20 s, faster than the smallest model on the CPU. On the CPU alone it takes about 3 s on one thread, which mostly falls inside the 3-second countdown before a message is sent. Q4_K_M scores the same as Q8_0 on the sets below at two thirds of the size; Q8_0's transcripts match the original model's more closely word for word, without a measurable difference in error rate. Each option below was timed on the same idle machine, on the CPU, and scored on the same LibriSpeech utterances.

| Model and runtime | Download | Time for 10 s of speech, 1 thread (lower is better) | CPU-seconds per second of speech (lower is better) | Peak memory | Load time | Word error rate, test-clean | Word error rate, test-other |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Parakeet TDT-CTC 110M, Q4_K_M, transcribe.cpp | 90 MB | 0.65 s | 0.066 | 0.31 GB | 0.24 s | 2.70% | 5.23% |
| Parakeet TDT-CTC 110M, int8, [sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx) | 108 MB | 0.42 s | 0.043 | 0.51 GB | 1.2 s | 2.39% | 5.23% |
| [parakeet-ft-v1](https://huggingface.co/freetranscribe/parakeet-ft-v1) (a long-form fine-tune of v3), Q4_K_M, transcribe.cpp (chosen) | 485 MB | 3.14 s | 0.306 | 1.08 GB | 1.2 s | 2.10% | 3.61% |
| Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 (25 languages), int8, sherpa-onnx | 640 MB | 1.83 s | 0.185 | 1.38 GB | 3.1 s | 2.68% | 4.69% |
| [Parakeet Redux](https://huggingface.co/moondream/parakeet-redux) (v3 with ternary weights), transcribe.cpp | 159 MB | 2.76 s | 0.279 | 0.63 GB | 0.4 s | 2.43% | 4.52% |

The same 110M model runs on both transcribe.cpp and sherpa-onnx. sherpa-onnx transcribes it faster, at 0.04 CPU-seconds per second of speech instead of 0.07, and finishes 10 seconds of speech 0.23 s sooner. transcribe.cpp uses 0.2 GB less memory, loads five times faster, which matters when gear unloads models while idle, and ships as about 5 MB of code where sherpa-onnx needs 30 MB with [ONNX Runtime](https://onnxruntime.ai/). The extra time falls inside the 3-second countdown before a message is sent.

The [Parakeet TDT-CTC 110M](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m) is the light option for a machine without a GPU: on the CPU it needs a fifth of parakeet-ft-v1's time and a third of its memory, at a word error rate within noise of v3's. gear runs any Parakeet GGUF in its models folder, such as the 110M's from [handy-computer/parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m-gguf](https://huggingface.co/handy-computer/parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m-gguf): `--hear-model FILE` picks one, a click on the model's name in the window's status bar switches to the next, and the choice is remembered.

Recognition for other languages is not decided. Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 is the measured candidate; transcribe.cpp runs it as well, but only its sherpa-onnx build was timed.

#### How the speech recognition options were measured

Timing used 57 utterances (460 seconds) from [LibriSpeech](https://www.openslr.org/12) test-clean and test-other, transcribed one at a time with the model loaded once, on an idle Ryzen 7 3800XT (Zen 2 with AVX2, 8 cores) under Fedora 44 in WSL2. The time for 10 s of speech is the median over utterances of encoder plus decoder time, scaled to 10 seconds of audio. Peak memory is the process's peak resident set size; the sherpa-onnx rows ran from a Python script, which adds about 40 MB.

Accuracy used every tenth utterance of test-clean (262 utterances, 5,521 words) and test-other (294 utterances, 5,483 words), normalized with the Whisper English text normalizer that the [Open ASR Leaderboard](https://huggingface.co/spaces/hf-audio/open_asr_leaderboard) uses and scored with [jiwer](https://github.com/jitsi/jiwer). Against Parakeet v3, the chosen model differs by +0.02 percentage points on test-clean (95% interval -0.42 to +0.45) and +0.55 on test-other (-0.11 to +1.22), from a bootstrap that resamples speakers, since utterances from one speaker are not independent.

Versions: transcribe.cpp at commit `90cd63d`, a CPU build whose command-line program, with every model family it supports, is 5.2 MB; sherpa-onnx 1.13.8. The Redux row ran on [NairoDorian/transcribe.cpp](https://github.com/NairoDorian/transcribe.cpp) at `692350d`, a fork that adds the ternary weight type Redux needs.

LibriSpeech is read audiobook speech. How these models handle coding vocabulary, background noise, and a reply playing through the loudspeakers has not been measured yet.

### Models run on the GPU where one is usable

gear is built with ggml's [Vulkan](https://www.vulkan.org/) backend, so both models run on the GPU of any vendor whose driver supports Vulkan, and on the CPU where there is no GPU, no Vulkan driver, or `--cpu` was given. The GPU makes the larger, more accurate recognition models cheap: on an RTX 3080, parakeet-ft-v1 transcribes 10 s of speech in 0.20 s, three times faster than the 110M model on one CPU thread, and Pocket starts speaking 10 to 50 ms after a reply is queued instead of about 100 ms.

Times on a Windows 11 PC with a Ryzen 7 3800XT and an RTX 3080, recognition on one CPU thread and synthesis on two, the models loaded once:

| Work | GPU (RTX 3080) | CPU |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| parakeet-ft-v1 Q4_K_M, time for 10 s of speech | 0.20 s | 2.97 s |
| parakeet-ft-v1 Q4_K_M, slowest of 57 utterances | 0.49 s | |
| Parakeet TDT-CTC 110M Q4_K_M, time for 10 s of speech | 0.10 s | 0.69 s |
| Pocket TTS, time to first audio | 0.01 to 0.05 s | 0.07 to 0.13 s |
| Pocket TTS, time for 6 s of speech | 0.14 s | 1.64 s |
| Load, parakeet-ft-v1 | 0.50 s | 0.34 s |

The GPU gives the same accuracy: on the 556 LibriSpeech utterances used for accuracy above, parakeet-ft-v1 scores 2.10% and 3.54% on the GPU against 2.10% and 3.61% on the CPU, and the 110M model 2.70% and 5.27% against 2.64% and 5.16%; the transcripts differ only where rounding tips a word.

The first run after a GPU driver is installed or updated compiles the shaders for that GPU: the first piece of speech then took 15 s and the first reply 2.9 s to start. The driver keeps what it compiled, and later runs start at the speeds above.

The cost is size: the compiled shaders add 44 MB to the program, which compresses to 8 MB with zstd. The Vulkan library is opened when gear starts rather than linked, so the same program starts on a machine without it. On Linux, gear on a machine without a GPU, and in a container without the Vulkan library, ran on the CPU with the same transcripts.

### Speech detection: Silero VAD at 0.5

The detector decides when the user has started talking. Its mistakes cost twice: a false start stops a reply being read, and the piece it cuts may be transcribed into words that were never said. gs_voice's detector, the small network WebRTC's gain control uses, took breathing and room noise for speech: of 247 pieces gear kept in daily use, 239 had no words in them. Silero VAD, run through hearsay on transcribe.cpp, rejects almost all of that and catches as many short words.

Each detector's scores for the same recordings were replayed through gear's listener, counting the recordings in which a piece of speech starts (lower is better for the first two columns, higher for the last two):

| Detector and rule to start a piece | Kept pieces with no words (240) | Background noise (408) | One-word recordings (4,074) | LibriSpeech sentences (556) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| gs_voice, 0.8 held for 30 ms (before) | 65.0% | 39.0% | 98.9% | 100% |
| gs_voice, 0.8 held for 100 ms | 7.9% | 32.6% | 95.4% | 100% |
| Silero, 0.5 held for two 32 ms windows (chosen) | 5.0% | 0.5% | 99.2% | 100% |
| Silero, 0.7 held for two 32 ms windows | 1.2% | 0.0% | 98.8% | 100% |

Holding gs_voice's start longer drops the breaths but also misses short words such as "up" and "stop", and takes a third of the background noise for speech whatever the rule. Silero at 0.7 rejects a little more, but it was slower to notice the user talking over a reply played through loudspeakers: 0.11 to 0.24 s against 0.11 to 0.17 s at 0.5. Silero costs about 0.06 ms of CPU for each 32 ms (transcribe.cpp's measurement on an Apple M4) and adds a 1.2 MB model; gs_voice still cancels the echo.

How it was measured: each recording was played with 0.5 s of silence before and 1 s after through a fresh detector, one score per 10 ms frame for gs_voice and per 32 ms window for Silero (the Python [silero-vad](https://pypi.org/project/silero-vad/) package, 6.2), and gear's listener was replayed over the scores. The kept pieces are the ones gs_voice had started on and Parakeet found no words in, so that column favours any other detector; the background noise is the 408 one-second clips of noise in the test set of Google's [Speech Commands](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03209) v0.02, whose one-word recordings of its ten command words (yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go) make the word column; the sentences are the LibriSpeech utterances used for accuracy above.

### Speech or another sound of the voice: a score from both detectors and the recognizer

A throat clear reached an agent as the message "Okay". The voice detector starts a piece for a throat clear, a cough, a sigh or a laugh as it does for a word, and Parakeet writes words into some of those pieces. Which signals tell them from speech? At a setting that drops 2% of speech, Silero's own score lets 70% of such pieces through, and a model over Silero's score, gs_voice's and Parakeet's raw scores lets 22% through. The comparison is on pieces cut by gear's own listener from public recordings, with each method scored on speakers it was not fitted on.

| Told apart by | Other sounds of the voice let through when 2% of speech is dropped |
| --- | ---: |
| Silero alone: the time its score spent at 0.95 or more | 70% |
| Logistic regression over both detectors and Parakeet's reported confidence | 49% |
| Logistic regression over all 17 inputs | 37% |
| Boosted trees over all 17 inputs, 100 trees of 15 leaves | 22% |

The speech is [Speech Commands](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03209)' test set of single words and 1,000 read sentences of [LibriSpeech](https://www.openslr.org/12) test-clean (both CC BY 4.0), 5,664 pieces with words. The other sounds are [VocalSound](https://github.com/YuanGongND/vocalsound) (CC BY-SA 4.0), 21,024 recordings of coughs, throat clearing, sighs, sneezes, sniffs and laughter, of which Parakeet wrote words into 4,968 pieces, such as "uh", "um", "oh", "yeah" and "ha ha". The trees are fitted with [LightGBM](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/) by `tools/fit-speech-model`, which writes them as a table in `src/speech_model.h`; `gear hear FILE --keep DIR` writes each piece's inputs to `DIR/heard.jsonl`, the rows the tool is fitted on.

The first model, fitted on those recordings alone, dropped speech in real use: of 81 pieces of three words or more from the user's own microphone, kept from October 17 to November 12, it scored 20 under its cut, among them "Can you hear me now?" said over music and sentences said across the room. Silero, whose time at 0.99 or more was that model's largest input, rarely reached it on them: it calls music not speech, and none of the recordings had music, echo or distance in them. So each recording is used a second time as heard in a room, by `tools/room-mix`: with 70% chance it is put 0.5 to 4 m from the microphone in a random room, with 60% instrumental music from [MUSAN](https://www.openslr.org/17/) is mixed under it at -5 to 15 dB below it, with 30% noise from MUSAN at 5 to 20 dB, and its level is set between a distant talk and a shout. Speech and the other sounds get the same treatment, so the mix says nothing of which is which. Of 5,482 words and sentences mixed so, Parakeet wrote words into 4,819 pieces, and of 6,000 of VocalSound's recordings into 394. Fitted on both sets, the model drops 1 of the 81 real pieces ("is interpreted pollen can technically hold"), and of the 32 shorter ones "uh" and "she"; "Can you hear me now?" scores 0.98. The cost is in the other sounds: 33% get through against 22%, and of those heard in a room 66%.

By kind, the shipped model lets through 31% of throat clearing, 40% of coughs, 36% of sighs, 41% of sneezes, 40% of sniffs and 8% of laughter, and it drops 2.3% of single words, 2.2% of speech heard in a room and 0.1% of sentence pieces.

The inputs are the share of a piece's speech frames that Silero scored at 0.8, 0.9, 0.95 and 0.99 or more, with its peak and mean; the same for gs_voice's detector; and Parakeet's scores for the word pieces it wrote, taken before the softmax through hearsay: the chosen piece's score, its margin over writing nothing and over the next best piece, the log-sum-exp, its probability and the reported confidence. Of the trees' gain, 38% comes from Parakeet's margin over writing nothing, 12% from gs_voice's peak and 11% from Parakeet's confidence. Since the inputs overlap, leaving a group out says more: fitted without Parakeet's six scores the trees let 63% through, without gs_voice's five 47%, and without Silero's six 40%. The reported confidence alone, which is computed after the softmax, gave a throat clear's "Okay" 0.84 and spoken ones 0.76 to 0.93. No input counts frames or words. A first model had the time above each threshold and the number of word pieces among its inputs and was fitted on single words alone; it dropped every one of 44 pieces of six words or more from real use, having learned that a long sound is not a word.

To fit again: `tools/room-mix mix` writes the mixed recordings, `gear hear FILE --keep DIR` cuts and scores each as gear does live, `tools/room-mix rows` turns what was kept into rows, and `tools/fit-speech-model` fits on those with the rows of the recordings as they are.
